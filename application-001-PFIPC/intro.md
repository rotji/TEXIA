# TEXIA TEST 001

## Phase 02 — Information Segmentation

### Our rule

For every source, we ask:

> **What exactly is this source giving us?**

Not:

> “What is the story?”

We classify each piece as one of:

```text
O = Observation
C = Claim
EV = Evidence
EVT = Event
Q = Qualification
I = Inference
H = Hypothesis
U = Unknown
```

And we preserve the source:

```text
S001 → O001
S001 → C001
S001 → Q001
...
```

# 03.2 — Separate Entity Identity From Legal Status

This is another important distinction.

Consider:

> PFIPC was not established through law, executive order, or valid government instrument.

That statement concerns **legal/institutional status**.

It does **not necessarily mean**:

Those are different propositions.

We therefore need to separate:

```text

       ↓

       ↓

OPERATIONAL STATUS
       ↓
Did people actually operate under that name?

REPRESENTATIONAL STATUS
       ↓
Did people present PFIPC as a government institution?

INSTITUTIONAL RECOGNITION
       ↓
Did other institutions treat it as legitimate?
```

These can have different answers.

That is a major intelligence distinction.

# 03.4 — Resolve the Appointment Event

Now consider the appointment question.

There are potentially several different claims:

### C001

> Adeyemi portrayed himself as appointed by the Chief of Staff.

This is a claim about **Adeyemi's representation**.

### C002

> The Chief of Staff says no appointment was made.

This is an **institutional assertion/denial**.

### C003

> Adeyemi was not appointed by the federal government.

This is an **investigative finding** attributed to ICPC.

These are **not three versions of the same sentence**.

They are three epistemically different objects concerning the same underlying proposition.

We therefore model:

```text
              APPOINTMENT PROPOSITION
                       │
        ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
        ↓              ↓              ↓
   ADEYEMI'S        CHIEF OF       ICPC
   REPRESENTATION   STAFF          FINDING
                    DENIAL
        │              │              │
       C001           C002           C003
```

This is much stronger than simply saying:

> "Sources disagree."

Because at this point they may not actually disagree.

The sources could be describing:

1. what Adeyemi represented;
2. what the office officially denied;
3. what investigators subsequently established.

# 03.5 — Test Claim Resolution

Now we ask:

> Are C001, C002 and C003 contradictory?

Not necessarily.

### C001

> Adeyemi represented that he had been appointed.

### C002

> The Chief of Staff says no appointment was made.

These can simultaneously be true.

There is no contradiction.

### C003

> ICPC found that Adeyemi was not appointed by the federal government.

This is consistent with C002.

So:

```text
C001
"Person represented X"

        ≠

"X actually happened"
```

This is a critical TEXIA rule.

A source reporting that somebody **claimed something** is evidence that the claim was made.

It is not automatically evidence that the underlying proposition is true.

# 03.8 — New Test: Relationship Strength Ladder

Test 001 has now exposed another useful conceptual structure.

Relationships have different evidentiary strengths.

For example:

```text
MENTIONED
   ↓
ASSOCIATED WITH
   ↓
LINKED TO
   ↓
AFFILIATED WITH
   ↓
REPRESENTED BY
   ↓
OPERATED BY
   ↓
CONTROLLED BY
   ↓
OWNED BY
```

But this should **not** be treated as a universal hierarchy where one relationship automatically implies another.

For example:

> linked to

does not mean:

> owned by.

And:

> associated with

does not mean:

> controlled by.

So this is not merely a "strength ladder."

It is better understood as a:

> **Relationship Semantic Map**

Different relationships encode different kinds of connection.

That is an important distinction.

For example:

```text

HIGH
```

does **not** mean:

```text

```

A highly confident "linked to" relationship remains a "linked to" relationship.

Confidence cannot transform one relationship type into another.

This is sufficiently general that I would add it to our **V0.1 Amendment file**, but **not yet call it V0.2**.

# PHASE 04.2 — EVENT TIME vs PUBLICATION TIME vs DISCOVERY TIME vs EFFECTIVE TIME

> **The date attached to a document is not necessarily the date of the event described by the document.**

## 04.2.1 — One Information Object Can Contain Multiple Times

Take a hypothetical source published on **August 8** saying:

> An investigation established that an individual was not appointed by the government.

There are potentially several different times:

```text
T1 = Appointment event
     ↓
When the alleged appointment supposedly occurred

T2 = Document creation
     ↓
When the appointment document was created

T3 = Document use
     ↓
When the document was presented/used

T4 = Discovery
     ↓
When investigators discovered relevant evidence

T5 = Investigation
     ↓
When investigators examined the matter

T6 = Finding
     ↓
When investigators reached the finding

T7 = Publication
     ↓
When the finding was publicly reported

T8 = Effective time
     ↓
When an action resulting from the finding took effect
```

These can all be different.

Therefore:

```text
PUBLICATION DATE ≠ EVENT DATE
```

# 04.2.2 — Apply This to Test 001

Consider the June 11 State House disclaimer.

We know:

```text

11 June 2026
```

But what does that date actually tell us?

It establishes that:

> On June 11, the State House publicly communicated its position.

It does **not** establish:

* when Adeyemi first represented himself as appointed;
* when the purported appointment occurred;
* when PFIPC-related activity began;
* when any document was created;

```text
11 June 2026

Temporal precision:
DAY
```

The unknown field is important.

We do **not** invent a date.

# 04.2.3 — Publication Time

Publication time answers:

> **When did this source become available to an audience?**

Examples:

* newspaper publication;
* government press release;
* committee report;
* ICPC announcement;
* social-media statement;
* court judgment publication.

It belongs to the **source/document event**, not automatically to the underlying event being described.

Example:

```text
Document D001

Publication:
11 June 2026

Claim inside D001:
"No appointment was made."

Underlying appointment event:
UNKNOWN
```

This distinction allows TEXIA to correctly process historical claims.

# 04.2.4 — Discovery Time

Now consider an investigation.

Suppose something happened in March.

Investigators discover evidence of it in July.

The discovery date is:

```text
July
```

The event date remains:

```text
March
```

Therefore:

```text
EVENT
March
   ↓
EVIDENCE EXISTS
   ↓
DISCOVERED
July
   ↓
REPORTED
August
```

If TEXIA used the document date as the event date, it would incorrectly reconstruct history.

This is one of the easiest ways an intelligence system can produce a false timeline while using perfectly accurate source dates.

# 04.2.5 — Effective Time

Effective time is different again.

Consider a government decision:

> A suspension was announced on August 10, effective August 15.

We therefore have:

```text
Announcement:
10 August

Effective:
15 August
```

The announcement changed the **knowledge state** on August 10.

The suspension changed the relevant **operational state** on August 15.

Therefore:

```text
ANNOUNCEMENT
      ↓
KNOWLEDGE STATE CHANGE

EFFECTIVE DATE
      ↓
WORLD / INSTITUTIONAL STATE CHANGE
```

This distinction becomes extremely important for:

* laws;
* regulations;
* appointments;
* dismissals;
* sanctions;
* contracts;
* policies;
* financial decisions;
* organizational changes.

---

# 04.2.6 — Reference Time

There is another temporal concept.

A source published today may describe something that happened years ago.

Example:

> "The organization was established in 2020."

If the article was published in 2026:

```text
Publication time = 2026

Event/reference time = 2020
```

Therefore TEXIA needs to preserve:

```text
SOURCE TIME
      ≠
REFERENCED EVENT TIME
```

This is already consistent with our earlier Temporal Binding work.

# 04.2.7 — Relative Temporal Expressions

Now things get harder.

Sources frequently use expressions such as:

* yesterday
* last week
* earlier this year
* previously
* recently
* shortly afterwards
* months later
* before the investigation
* after the announcement
* at the time
* subsequently
* meanwhile
* during the period

These cannot always be converted into exact dates.

For example:

> "The company had previously received approval."

If the document is dated September 2026, "previously" only tells us:

```text
Approval < publication/reference point
```

It does not tell us:

```text
Approval = 14 March 2026
```

unless additional evidence establishes that date.

This reinforces our existing:

> **Temporal Precision Principle**

# 04.2.8 — Temporal Intervals

Not every event happens at one moment.

Some events have duration.

For example:

> An investigation lasted from July to August.

This should be:

```text

August
```

rather than:

```text

August
```

Likewise:

* a policy period;
* a conflict;
* a financial position;
* an organizational role;
* a regulatory status

may exist over an interval.

# 04.2.9 — Persistent States

This reveals another distinction.

An **event** happens.

A **state** persists.

For example:

```text

        ↓
```

```text
Person no longer holds position
```

```text

```

```text

```

This is more useful than storing a simple "appointed = yes/no."

# PHASE 04.3 — IDENTIFY STATE-CHANGING EVENTS

Now we test a critical part of TEXIA:

> **Did the event actually change the state of the world, or did it merely change what people knew about the state?**

This distinction is essential because otherwise TEXIA could mistake **news about a change** for **the change itself**.

---

## 04.3.1 — What Is a State Change?

A state change occurs when the condition of the subject changes.

Basic model:

```text id="3w3g4x"
PREVIOUS STATE
      ↓
     EVENT
      ↓
CURRENT STATE
```

For example:

```text id="3b2jcy"
Person does not hold position
          ↓
       Appointment
          ↓
Person holds position
```

The appointment is a **state-changing event**.

But:

```text id="2y6v8d"
Person holds position
          ↓
Newspaper reports appointment
          ↓
Person holds position
```

The newspaper article does **not** itself change the person's position.

It changes the **knowledge state** of readers.

---

# 04.3.2 — Apply This to Test 001

Let's examine the June 11 disclaimer.

### Event

```text
State House issues disclaimer
```

Did this necessarily change the legal status of PFIPC?

No.

The disclaimer may have **revealed or reaffirmed** the government's position.

So:

```text id="kj9y3c"
PFIPC LEGAL STATUS
        │
        │ possibly unchanged
        │
        ↓
STATE HOUSE DISCLOSURE
        │
        ↓
KNOWLEDGE STATE CHANGES
```

# 04.3.7 — Revelation vs Transformation

We can now distinguish two broad event classes.

### Transformation Event

An event that changes the underlying state.

```text id="u2v9rf"

  ↓

  ↓
```

### Revelation Event

An event that changes what is known about the state.

```text id="1s5kwc"

     │
     │ unchanged
     ↓

     ↓
```

### Response Event

An event that changes an institutional response.

```text id="wh4wqv"

        ↓
INSTITUTIONAL DECISION
        ↓
RESPONSE STATE CHANGES
```

These three should not be conflated.

# TEXIA TEST 001 — PHASE 04.8

## Causality vs Sequence

We now test whether TEXIA can avoid one of the most dangerous analytical errors:

> **A happened before B, therefore A caused B.**

That inference is invalid unless we have evidence for a causal mechanism.

---

# 1. Start with the PFIPC timeline

We currently have:

```text
June 11
State House disclaimer
        ↓
July 7
Presidential investigation order
        ↓
July 21
Investigative engagement
        ↓
July 24
Budget clarification
        ↓
August 8
ICPC interim finding
        ↓
September 2
House committee findings
```

This establishes **temporal order**.

It does not automatically establish:

```text
June 11 caused July 7
July 7 caused July 21
July 21 caused July 24
July 24 caused August 8
August 8 caused September 2
```

We need another analytical layer.

---

# 2. The simplest causal mistake

Consider:

```text
A happened
   ↓
B happened later
```

The only thing we know from this is:

```text
A BEFORE B
```

We do **not** yet know:

```text
A CAUSED B
```

There could be:

```text
A ───────→ B
```

but also:

```text
C ───────→ A
C ───────→ B
```

or:

```text
A       B
 \     /
   C
```

or:

```text
A
↓
D
↓
B
```

or simply:

```text
A       B
```

with no meaningful causal relationship.

---

# 3. Apply this to June 11 → July 7

We know:

```text
June 11
Public disclaimer
        ↓
July 7
Investigation ordered
```

Could the June 11 disclaimer have contributed to the investigation?

**Possibly.**

Can TEXIA currently state:

> “The disclaimer caused the investigation”?

**No.**

Why?

Because we would need evidence about the decision pathway.

For example:

```text
June 11 disclaimer
        ↓
Internal escalation
        ↓
Evidence submitted
        ↓
Presidential review
        ↓
Decision
        ↓
July 7 investigation order
```

Without evidence for such a pathway, causation remains a hypothesis.

---

# 4. Trigger vs cause

This introduces an important distinction.

An event can be a:

* background condition;
* contributing factor;
* trigger;
* immediate cause;
* enabling condition;
* mechanism;
* necessary condition;
* sufficient condition.

These are not interchangeable.

Suppose the June 11 disclaimer triggered a review.

That does not necessarily mean it was the **ultimate cause** of the investigation.

We might have:

```text
Underlying concerns
       ↓
Earlier evidence
       ↓
Institutional escalation
       ↓
June 11 disclaimer
       ↓
Further developments
       ↓
July 7 investigation
```

June 11 may be a **triggering event** within a larger causal chain.

---

# 5. Causal chain vs chronological chain

TEXIA should therefore maintain two separate structures.

### Timeline

```text
A → B → C → D
```

means:

```text
A happened before B
B happened before C
C happened before D
```

### Causal graph

```text
A ──→ C
B ──→ C
C ──→ D
```

means something much stronger:

```text
A contributed to C
B contributed to C
C contributed to D
```

The two graphs should never be confused.

---

# 6. PFIPC example

Let's hypothesize:

```text
June 11 disclaimer
        ↓
July 7 investigation
```

The evidence currently supports:

```text
TEMPORAL RELATIONSHIP
June 11 BEFORE July 7
```

But causal status should be:

```text
CAUSAL RELATIONSHIP
UNKNOWN
```

That is the correct TEXIA representation.

---

Suppose we discover a document saying:

> Following the June 11 disclaimer, the President directed the ICPC to investigate the matter.

Now we have evidence of a causal pathway.

We might upgrade:

```text

        ↓

        ↓

```

But even then, we need to distinguish:

```text
TRIGGER
```

from:

```text
SOLE CAUSE
```

The document might establish that the disclaimer triggered the decision without establishing that it was the only reason.

---

```text

```

Or:

```text
Causal relation:
UNRESOLVED
```

# 9. The counterfactual test

One useful causal question is:

> **If A had not happened, would B probably still have happened?**

For example:

> If the June 11 disclaimer had never occurred, would the July 7 investigation still have occurred?

We currently cannot answer.

Possible worlds:

```text
WORLD 1
No disclaimer
   ↓
No investigation

WORLD 2
No disclaimer
   ↓
Investigation anyway

WORLD 3
No disclaimer
   ↓
Investigation delayed

WORLD 4
No disclaimer
   ↓
Different investigation
```

Without evidence, we should not select one.

This is where **causal inference becomes different from ordinary temporal reasoning.**

---

# 10. Mechanism matters

Strong causal analysis needs a plausible mechanism.

For example:

```text
Event A
   ↓
Mechanism M
   ↓
Outcome B
```

Suppose:

```text
Public disclaimer
       ↓
raises institutional concern
       ↓
internal escalation
       ↓
presidential decision
       ↓
ICPC investigation
```

The mechanism explains **how** A could contribute to B.

Without a mechanism, the causal assertion is weaker.

---

# 11. Alternative explanations

TEXIA should actively search for alternatives.

Suppose the investigation occurred on July 7.

Possible explanations:

```text
H1:
June 11 disclaimer triggered investigation.

H2:
Earlier evidence independently triggered investigation.

H3:
Banking evidence triggered investigation.

H4:
Diplomatic concerns triggered investigation.

H5:
Multiple factors jointly triggered investigation.

H6:
The June 11 event was coincidental to the decision.
```

The system should not automatically choose H1 merely because it is temporally convenient.

---

# 12. Confounding

We now encounter another important concept.

Suppose:

```text
A = June 11 disclaimer
B = July 7 investigation
C = previously discovered evidence
```

If C influenced both A and B:

```text
        C
       / \
      ↓   ↓
     A     B
```

then the apparent:

```text
A → B
```

relationship could be misleading.

This is a **confounding problem**.

TEXIA therefore needs to distinguish:

```text
Observed sequence
```

from:

```text
Potential causal structure
```

---

# 13. Causal direction can also be reversed

Another danger:

```text
A happened before B
```

does not guarantee:

```text
A caused B
```

But sometimes B may influence our interpretation of A.

For example:

```text
Earlier event
     ↓
Later investigation
     ↓
New evidence
     ↓
Reinterpretation of earlier event
```

The later finding doesn't travel backward in time and change the historical event.

It changes our **knowledge about the historical event**.

So:

```text
CAUSAL TIME
```

and:

```text
KNOWLEDGE TIME
```

can move differently.

---

Imagine September 2 reveals numerous bank accounts.

Impossible chronologically.

But a careless analytical system could accidentally create such relationships when generating narratives.

The correct representation is:

```text

Account activity
       ↓
Later:
Committee discovers account activity
```

The September event explains our **knowledge**, not the creation of the historical activity.

That's a much safer intelligence object.

## 1. World–Knowledge–Response State Separation

This is the largest gap exposed by Test 001.

TEXIA originally had a general concept of **state**, but that is too broad.

We now need three fundamental state layers:

```text
                 INFORMATION
                      │
          ┌───────────┼───────────┐
          ↓           ↓           ↓
       WORLD       KNOWLEDGE    RESPONSE
       STATE        STATE        STATE
          │           │           │
          ↓           ↓           ↓
      What was      What was     What did
      happening?    known?       institutions do?
```

### World State

What was actually occurring or existing.

Examples:

* whether an entity legally existed
* whether an appointment actually occurred
* whether money was released
* whether an account existed
* whether an event occurred

### Knowledge State

What actors knew, believed, discovered, reported, or established.

Examples:

* State House became aware of alleged impersonation
* investigators discovered bank accounts
* ICPC established certain findings
* journalists reported a development

### Response State

What institutions did in response.

Examples:

* disclaimer issued
* investigation ordered
* committee hearing conducted
* documents requested
* agencies notified

### Gap

Without this separation:

> discovery can be incorrectly represented as creation.

```text
INVESTIGATION TIME
DECISION TIME
EFFECTIVE TIME
REFERENCE TIME
```

For example:

```text
Historical condition
      │
      ├── existed in 2025
      │
      ├── discovered in 2026
      │
      ├── investigated in 2026
      │
      ├── finding made in 2026
      │
      └── reported publicly in 2026
```

These must not collapse into one timestamp.

### New requirement

TEXIA must distinguish:

> **When something happened**
> from
> **when someone learned about it**
> from
> **when someone reported it**.

# 3. State Dimension Principle

A state change must specify **which dimension changed**.

We now have at least:

```text
LEGAL
ADMINISTRATIVE
OPERATIONAL
FINANCIAL
KNOWLEDGE
INVESTIGATIVE
INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE
REPRESENTATIONAL
PUBLIC-KNOWLEDGE
```

Example:

### July 7 investigation order

It changes:

```text
INVESTIGATIVE STATE
RESPONSE STATE
KNOWLEDGE STATE
```

But it does **not automatically change**:

```text
LEGAL EXISTENCE
```

This is critical.

# 4. State Transition Verification

We need to prevent TEXIA from declaring a state transition merely because an event was reported.

A genuine reconstructed transition should ideally look like:

```text
BEFORE STATE
     │
     ↓
STATE-CHANGING EVENT
     │
     ↓
AFTER STATE
```

And each component should have evidence.

For example:

```text
Budget State

Before:
No appropriation established

        ↓
National Assembly appropriation

        ↓

After:
Appropriation exists

BUT

Financial Release:
UNKNOWN / NO RELEASE ESTABLISHED
```

Therefore:

> **One state transition does not imply transitions in other state dimensions.**

# 5. Revelation–Transformation Separation

This emerged repeatedly.

TEXIA needs to explicitly distinguish:

```text
TRANSFORMATION
DISCOVERY
CONFIRMATION
RECLASSIFICATION
RESPONSE
PUBLICATION
```

For example:

```text
Bank account created
       ↓
Account exists
       ↓
Investigators discover account
       ↓
Committee reports discovery
       ↓
Public learns about account
```

Only the first is necessarily a **world-state transformation**.

The others are changes in knowledge or response.

### New principle

> **Revelation ≠ Transformation**

This should become a formal architectural rule.

# 6. Unknown Before-State

Another important discovery:

Sometimes we don't know the previous state.

That is not equivalent to:

```text
FALSE
```

We need:

```text
TRUE
FALSE
UNKNOWN
```

For example:

> “PFIPC activity existed before June 11.”

If we don't have sufficient evidence about its activity before June 11, TEXIA should represent:

```text
World State Before June 11:
UNKNOWN
```

Not:

```text
NO ACTIVITY
```

This is essential for historical reconstruction.

# 7. State Metadata

A state cannot simply be:

```text
Entity → State
```

It needs provenance.

Candidate structure:

```text
STATE
│
├── subject
├── dimension
├── value
├── as_of_time
├── valid_from
├── valid_until
├── evidence
├── source
├── provenance
├── epistemic_status
├── confidence
└── unresolved_components
```

This means TEXIA can say:

> “This was the state **as reconstructed from these sources**, as of this time.”

rather than pretending that its reconstruction is reality itself.

# 8. Temporal Graph ≠ Causal Graph

Phase 04.8 exposed another major architectural distinction.

We need at least three relationship structures:

```text
TEMPORAL GRAPH
     │
     ├── before
     ├── after
     ├── during
     ├── overlaps
     └── concurrent


CAUSAL GRAPH
     │
     ├── caused_by
     ├── contributed_to
     ├── enabled_by
     ├── triggered_by
     └── mechanism


EVIDENCE GRAPH
     │
     ├── supports
     ├── contradicts
     ├── derived_from
     └── corroborates
```

The system must never infer:

```text
A → B temporally
```

as:

```text
A → B causally
```

# 9. Causal Relationship Metadata

A causal link needs substantially more structure than:

```text
A caused B
```

Candidate model:

```text
CAUSAL RELATIONSHIP
│
├── cause
├── effect
├── mechanism
├── cause_time
├── effect_time
├── evidence
├── alternatives
├── scope
├── confidence
└── status
```

Status:

```text
UNKNOWN
POSSIBLE
UNRESOLVED
SUPPORTED
STRONGLY_SUPPORTED
REJECTED
```

This prevents:

> “A happened before B”

from becoming:

> “A caused B.”

# 10. Contradiction Normalization

Phase 04.7 revealed that contradiction detection cannot operate directly on raw sentences.

Before testing contradiction:

```text

```

TEXIA must normalize:

```text

PREDICATE

SCOPE
```

Only then:

```text

```

Therefore:

> **Contradiction is a property of comparable normalized propositions, not merely different sentences.**

# 12. Missing Transition Detection

This is particularly powerful.

Suppose TEXIA sees:

```text

Entity does not have authorization.

SOURCE B:
Entity is operating with apparent authorization.
```

The naïve system says:

> CONTRADICTION.

The better system asks:

```text
Was there a transition between A and B?
```

Possibly:

```text
A
│
│ UNKNOWN EVENT
↓
B
```

So TEXIA should be capable of detecting:

> **There may be an unobserved transition event between two apparently conflicting states.**

This creates a new investigative capability.

```text

     ↓
     ?
     ↓
KNOWN STATE
```

The `?` becomes an:

> **Open Transition**

which generates an investigation question.

# 13. State vs Status

Another subtle gap.

These should not be identical.

### State

What the world/system is believed to be like.

Example:

```text

```

### Status

Where the investigation/process currently stands.

Example:

```text

```

An investigation being:

```text

```

does not mean the underlying state is:

```text
UNKNOWN
```

and vice versa.

So:

```text
STATE ≠ STATUS
```

# 16. New Principles Exposed by Phase 04

We can now formally identify the likely amendments.

### Amendment candidates

**008 — World–Knowledge–Response State Separation Principle**

> TEXIA must distinguish changes in the underlying world state from changes in knowledge and institutional response.

**009 — Temporal Dimension Separation Principle**

> TEXIA must represent distinct temporal dimensions independently and must not substitute publication, discovery, investigation, decision, reference, or effective time for event time.

**010 — State Dimension Principle**

> Every reconstructed state and state transition must identify the dimension to which it applies.

**011 — State Transition Verification Principle**

> A state transition requires evidence for the relevant before-state, transition event, and after-state where available; unknown must remain distinct from false.

**012 — Revelation–Transformation Separation Principle**

> Discovery, confirmation, publication, reclassification, and institutional response must not be treated as underlying-world transformations unless evidence establishes an actual transformation.

**013 — State Metadata & Provenance Principle**

> Reconstructed states must preserve temporal validity, evidence, provenance, epistemic status, confidence, and unresolved components.

**014 — Temporal–Causal Graph Separation Principle**

> Temporal relationships and causal relationships must be represented independently; temporal sequence alone cannot establish causation.

**015 — Causal Relationship Qualification Principle**

> Causal relationships must preserve mechanism, alternatives, scope, evidence, confidence, and causal status.

**016 — Contradiction Normalization Principle**

> Contradiction detection must occur only after relevant entities, claims, relationships, states, times, scopes, and epistemic statuses have been normalized and compared.

**017 — Missing Transition Detection Principle**

> Apparent contradictions between states should trigger investigation for possible unobserved transition events before being classified as genuine contradiction.

**018 — State–Status Separation Principle**

> The state of the subject must remain distinct from the status of the investigation, process, or institutional response concerning that subject.

# 18. Most Important Phase 04 Lesson

The central lesson is:

> **TEXIA must reconstruct not only what information says happened, but which kind of change that information represents.**

A document can tell us about:

* a world-state change,
* discovery of an existing condition,
* institutional recognition,
* an investigation,
* a response,
* a change in public knowledge,
* a new interpretation,

```text

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓
IS THERE A CAUSAL EXPLANATION?
        ↓
WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN?
```
