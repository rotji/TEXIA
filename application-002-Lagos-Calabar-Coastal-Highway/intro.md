## Alignment and Demolition Investigation

This is our first major **competing-claims investigation**.

The central question is:

> **Did the Lagos section of the highway change from an earlier planned alignment, and if so, why and what consequences followed?**

### 1. There is evidence of an alignment change

In April–May 2024, the government and affected communities were already discussing different alignments.

Community representatives in Okun-Ajah said the new alignment would cut through the existing village and asked the government to retain the earlier Lagos State plan. At the same time, the Federal Government began compensation payments to affected property owners. ([Radio Nigeria Lagos][1])

The Minister of Works subsequently stated that the government was using a **gazetted alignment**, while also describing a planned **25-kilometre realignment around Okun-Ajah to avoid submarine cables**. He gave different demolition implications for the alternative alignments: approximately 490 houses under the new alignment versus 750 under the gazetted route. ([Punch Newspapers][2])

So we have:

```text
EARLIER / ORIGINAL ROUTE
        ↓
PROPOSED NEW ALIGNMENT
        ↓
SUBMARINE-CABLE PROBLEM
        ↓
ROUTE ADJUSTMENT
        ↓
DIFFERENT PROPERTIES AFFECTED
```

But we need to be careful: **“original route,” “gazetted route,” “new alignment,” and “realignment” are not necessarily four completely different routes.** We need the actual engineering drawings and legal documents to establish exactly what each term refers to.

### 2. The environmental assessment gives us unusually valuable evidence

The official Environmental and Social Impact Assessment for Section 1 discusses the alignment issue directly.

It records that the project team considered a realignment and that concerns around the coastline and the potential destruction of ancestral homes at Okun-Ajah affected the decision. It also describes the proposed right-of-way and its relationship with other infrastructure such as the Lekki Deep Sea Port and Fourth Mainland Bridge. ([| Federal Ministry of Environment | EAD][3])

This is important because we now have something stronger than a newspaper claim:

> **The official environmental assessment itself contains evidence of route-design deliberation.**

### 3. Demolition

Demolition began in April 2024.

The Federal Government said compensation had been provided for affected structures, while affected property owners subsequently challenged the adequacy of the compensation. ([Channels Television][4])

The government later reported that compensation for Section 1 had increased from an initial **₦8 billion to ₦18 billion**. ([Tribune Online][5])

This gives us another state transition:

```text

        ↓

```

### 4. But compensation creates another competing claim

There are two very different propositions:

**Government position:**

> Compensation followed assessment and due process.

**Affected-property-owner position:**

> Compensation was substantially below the value of the properties and investments lost.

Those are **not automatically contradictory**.

The first concerns **the process used to determine compensation**.

The second concerns **whether the resulting amount was adequate**.

TEXIA therefore separates them:

```text

        ↓
DISPUTED

        ↓
REQUIRES FURTHER VERIFICATION
```

### 5. Another important finding

A later November 2024 report stated that the compensation requirement for Section 1 had risen to **₦18 billion**, while affected property owners continued to argue that compensation was inadequate. ([Tribune Online][5])

Therefore, we should **not investigate “compensation” as one question**.

We need four separate questions:

1. **How many properties were affected?**
2. **How were affected properties identified?**
3. **How was each property valued?**
4. **How much was actually paid to each affected owner?**

Only then can we assess the adequacy question.

## Test 002 — Lagos–Calabar Coastal Highway

### Alignment Reconstruction — First Pass

But we should **not yet assume these are four completely separate routes**. We need the actual maps/drawings to determine exactly where they differ.

---

# 1. What the government itself said

This is the most important evidence.

On May 24, 2024, the Minister of Works said:

* using the **old/gazetted alignment** would affect about 750 houses;
* using the **new alignment** would affect about 490 houses;
* there would be a realignment around Okun-Ajah;
* the reason given was avoiding submarine cables. ([Punch Newspapers][1])

Then, on June 12, 2024, the minister described the situation even more explicitly:

> the government had created a **new path** because of complaints concerning submarine cables and Okun-Ajah;

and said the route would move away from both the new and gazetted alignments before reconnecting with the new alignment around kilometre 25. ([Punch Newspapers][2])

That gives us an important reconstruction:

```text

          ↓
REJOIN NEW ALIGNMENT
around kilometre 25
```

So **“the alignment changed” is established.**

The more interesting question is now:

> **What physical portion changed, exactly, and what did that change do to people, infrastructure, cost and the project itself?**

---

# 2. The submarine-cable explanation is an important lead

The government did not merely say “we changed the route.”

The explanation was specific.

Stakeholders identified major telecommunications infrastructure around the Mopo area, including the **2Africa submarine cable** and **West Africa Cable System**, and argued that the proposed route presented an infrastructure risk. ([TheCable][3])

**The first four steps are supported by the record.**

The final step — exactly what additional property consequences resulted from the modified route — still needs reconstruction.

We need the underlying route documents.

# 6. We should therefore stop using "the new alignment"

This is an important methodological correction.

Whenever a source says:

> "new alignment"

we should record the phrase exactly as used by the source, but internally ask:

**New relative to what?**

Possible meanings:

* new relative to the 2006 gazetted alignment;
* new relative to the original project route;
* new relative to the first proposed engineering alignment;
* new relative to an earlier construction design;
* new relative to the route discussed at a stakeholders' meeting.

Therefore our record should be:

> **Source-described "new alignment"**

until its physical identity is established.

Likewise:

> **Source-described "old/gazetted alignment"**

rather than automatically assigning it to the ESIA's original alignment.

This prevents a major false merge.

```text
    ↓
```

```text
    ↓

    ↓
```

```text
    ↓
THE QUESTIONS
```
