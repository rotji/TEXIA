# 2. WHAT IS THIS PROJECT?

### Time

00:45–02:30

### Narration

The Lagos–Calabar Coastal Highway is a major federal road project intended to connect Lagos with communities and states along Nigeria's southern coastline toward Calabar.

The project did not begin as a construction site in 2024.

It had an earlier planning and development history, followed by formal government approval, contracting, financing arrangements and, eventually, physical construction.

For this investigation, those stages need to be kept separate.

A project can be approved without being constructed.

A contract can be awarded without the full contract value being spent.

Financing can be announced or closed without meaning that the same amount has already been paid to a contractor.

And a section can be operational while other sections remain under construction.

That distinction becomes important throughout this investigation.

# 3. HOW DID IT BEGIN?

### Time

02:30–05:00

### Narration

On 26 February 2024, the Federal Executive Council approved the construction of the Lagos–Port Harcourt–Calabar Coastal Superhighway project, with the first 47-kilometre section beginning in Lagos.

The first Lagos section was assigned to Hitech Construction Company Limited.

The section runs approximately 47.474 kilometres.

The approved contract value was approximately 1.068 trillion naira.

More precisely, the Federal Ministry of Works gives the contract figure as:

**₦1,067,887,381,148.61.**

The project was structured as an EPC plus Finance arrangement.

That structure is important because the financing mechanism was part of the project itself.

The project was then formally flagged off on 24 May 2024.

This is the point where the story moves from approval and contracting into physical implementation.

# 4. THE PROJECT DID NOT STAY STATIC

### Time

05:00–07:30

### Narration

But approval did not freeze the project in one fixed form.

The route and design went through changes.

The project's own environmental and social impact assessment documents multiple alignment alternatives for Section 1.

The documents describe an earlier proposed alignment and a later optimized alignment.

They also explain that alignment decisions considered existing infrastructure, traffic, environmental effects, community impacts and other constraints.

One important constraint was underground and submarine infrastructure, including telecommunications cables.

Public statements during 2024 also described disputes and changes around the Okun-Ajah area.

The government publicly described changes involving the older or gazetted route and a newer alignment, including a route adjustment intended to avoid submarine cables.

But this is where the evidence needs to be handled carefully.

The existence of multiple alignment alternatives is established.

The existence of an alignment dispute is established.

The exact relationship between the historical gazetted route, the engineering alternatives shown in the ESIA, the route described publicly as the "new alignment," and the final constructed centreline is **not yet completely established**.

That distinction matters.

A route changing during engineering development is not, by itself, evidence that something improper happened.

It is evidence that the project changed — and that the reason and consequences of those changes need to be reconstructed.

# 5. HOW WAS IT GOING TO BE PAID FOR?

### Time

07:30–10:00

### Narration

There was another major problem.

How would the project actually be financed?

The original structure expected the contractor to arrange financing.

But financing difficulties became part of the project's development.

By July 2025, financing for Phase 1 Section 1 reached financial close on a syndicated loan of approximately **$747 million**.

A separate financing package of approximately **$1.126 billion** was later associated with Phase 1 Section 2.

These figures tell us something important.

They tell us that financing arrangements existed.

But they do **not**, by themselves, tell us how much money had already been spent on construction.

That requires a different evidence trail.

A contract value is not the same thing as a loan.

A loan is not the same thing as a disbursement.

A disbursement is not necessarily the same thing as expenditure.

And expenditure is not automatically equivalent to the value of completed physical work.

This distinction becomes particularly important because a June 2025 statement reported that approximately 30 percent of the Section 1 contract sum had been disbursed.

That figure is important enough to investigate separately.

But at this stage, the underlying payment records needed to independently reconstruct the exact cash flow have not been located.

# 6. SOMETHING WAS ACTUALLY BUILT

### Time

10:00–13:00

### Narration

While questions remained about financing and design, physical construction was taking place.

The first Lagos section covers approximately 47.474 kilometres.

Approximately 30 kilometres of that section were commissioned in May 2025.

By December 2025, approximately 30 kilometres had been opened to traffic.

That does not mean the entire 47.474-kilometre Section 1 was complete.

The remaining portion continued to undergo construction.

Independent visual reporting in early 2026 also showed ongoing work on remaining parts of Section 1 and construction activity on Section 2.

And construction was not limited to Lagos.

Government and public reporting during 2026 documented construction activity in other parts of the wider project.

In Section 3B, approximately 4.5 kilometres of rigid pavement were reported completed by April 2026.

In August 2026, ministry updates described approximately 28 kilometres of concrete pavement work in Ogun and foundation works covering approximately 52 kilometres in Ondo.

Akwa Ibom also presents a more complicated picture, with different public descriptions of section lengths and progress.

So the evidence shows that this is not simply a Lagos road project.

It is a multi-section project progressing at different speeds and under different configurations.

# 7. THE NUMBERS NEED CONTEXT

### Time

13:00–15:00

### Narration

This brings us to one of the most confusing parts of the project.

The numbers.

Earlier official documents described the highway as approximately **700 kilometres**.

Later government descriptions have used approximately **750 kilometres**.

The section structure has also evolved.

And in September 2026, the Minister of Works clarified that a reported figure of approximately 70 percent referred to **Section 1**, rather than the entire Lagos–Calabar Coastal Highway.

That clarification is significant.

It demonstrates why a completion percentage cannot be understood without knowing its scope.

Seventy percent of one section is not seventy percent of the entire highway.

Likewise, a change from 700 to 750 kilometres cannot automatically be described as an error.

It could reflect a changed scope, alignment, measurement, section configuration or another project definition.

At this point, the evidence establishes that the numbers changed.

It does not yet fully establish **why** every number changed.

That is an unresolved part of the investigation.

# PRODUCTION RULES

## Rule 1 — Evidence Before Narration

No substantive sentence should survive into the final voice-over unless it can be connected to an evidence record.

```text
NARRATION
   ↓
CLAIM ID
   ↓
SOURCE ID
   ↓
EVIDENCE LOCATION
```

---

## Rule 2 — Source Lineage

Multiple articles repeating the same government statement should not automatically be counted as multiple independent confirmations.

```text
ORIGINAL STATEMENT
       ↓
ARTICLE A
       ↓
ARTICLE B
       ↓
ARTICLE C

= ONE INFORMATION LINEAGE
```

---

## Rule 3 — Temporal Discipline

Always distinguish:

* announcement date
* approval date
* contract date
* financing-close date
* disbursement date
* construction date
* commissioning date
* opening-to-traffic date
* current status date.

---

## Rule 4 — Scope Discipline

Never state:

> "The highway is 70% complete."

unless the evidence establishes that the figure applies to the entire highway.

Instead:

> "The minister said Section 1 was approximately 70% complete."

if that is what the source actually establishes.

---

## Rule 5 — Financial Discipline

Never equate:

```text
CONTRACT VALUE
FINANCING
DISBURSEMENT
EXPENDITURE
COMPLETED WORK
```

They are separate propositions requiring separate evidence.

---

## Rule 6 — Spatial Discipline

Do not visually combine:

* historical/gazetted alignment;
* ESIA First Proposed Alignment;
* ESIA Optimized Alignment;
* publicly described "new alignment";
* publicly described "old/gazetted alignment";
* constructed road;

into a single route unless the geometry has been verified.

---

## Rule 7 — Allegations

Claims made by:

* landowners;
* communities;
* contractors;
* politicians;
* government officials;
* commentators;
* media sources;

must be attributed appropriately unless independently established.

An allegation is not automatically a fact.

---

## Rule 8 — Unknowns

Unknowns should not be hidden simply because they interrupt the narrative.

Where a major question cannot currently be answered, say so.

---

## Rule 9 — Visual Integrity

Every visual must answer at least one of:

1. What happened?
2. Where did it happen?
3. When did it happen?
4. What evidence supports the statement?
5. What changed?
6. What remains uncertain?
