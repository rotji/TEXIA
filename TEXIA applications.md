Yes. Before choosing the first real-life test, it is useful to map the **full application space** of TEXIA.

And there is an important distinction: TEXIA is not itself a "finance tool," "regulatory tool," or "geopolitical tool." Those are **applications of the same underlying intelligence machinery**. Existing intelligence products already apply similar ideas to investment research, regulatory monitoring, supply-chain oversight, risk intelligence, due diligence, and portfolio monitoring. ([S&P Global][1])

So I would map TEXIA like this.

# But This Creates a Very Important Question

We **should not choose our first TEXIA test simply because an application sounds interesting**.

We should choose the application that gives us the **best stress test of the entire V0.1 architecture**.

Ideally, Test 001 should contain:

```text
Multiple sources
       +
Multiple entities
       +
Multiple events
       +
Conflicting claims
       +
Different timestamps
       +
Evidence of different quality
       +
State changes
       +
Uncertainty
       +
Potential inference
       +
A meaningful real-world consequence
```

That would allow **one real-world test to exercise almost the entire TEXIA engine**.

So I would next rank these application families by **how good they are as a first TEXIA laboratory**, rather than immediately picking whichever domain seems most commercially attractive.

# 1. OSINT / Investigative Intelligence — BEST FIRST TEST

This is my strongest recommendation.

Why?

Because it naturally forces TEXIA to deal with almost everything we've designed.

Imagine a real event.

We might have:

```text
Official statement
       +
News article
       +
Company statement
       +
Interview
       +
Government document
       +
Research/report
       +
Historical information
       +
Later correction
```

Now TEXIA has to figure out:

```text
WHO?
WHAT?
WHEN?
WHERE?
WHAT WAS CLAIMED?
WHAT WAS ACTUALLY OBSERVED?
WHAT SUPPORTS IT?
WHAT CONTRADICTS IT?
WHICH SOURCES ARE INDEPENDENT?
WHAT CHANGED?
WHAT IS STILL UNKNOWN?
```

That's almost the entire V0.1.

Modern OSINT workflows explicitly emphasize reconstructing events from fragmented sources into defensible timelines while preserving provenance and uncertainty. ([Cambridge Intelligence Developers][2])

* different perspectives
* changing relationships
* temporal sequences
* incomplete information
* uncertainty

The challenge is that geopolitical analysis can tempt us into **over-inference**:

> "Country A did X because it wants Y."

TEXIA would have to resist that unless evidence supports it.

That makes it a very good stress test of our **Observation → Inference → Hypothesis** separation.

**Verdict: Excellent.**

# 3. Regulatory / Legal Intelligence

This might actually be the **best test for precision**.

Consider:

```text
Draft regulation
      ↓
Consultation
      ↓
Revision
      ↓
Final regulation
      ↓
Effective date
      ↓
Guidance
      ↓
Enforcement
      ↓
Court challenge
      ↓
Amendment
```

This is almost a perfect TEXIA state machine.

It tests:

* document versions
* claims
* temporal precision
* effective dates
* entities
* relationships
* contradictions
* state transitions
* provenance

And regulatory-intelligence systems already use monitoring, analysis, review and knowledge workflows across complex regulatory environments. ([Policy-Insider.AI][3])

**Verdict: Extremely strong laboratory.**

# 4. Corporate / Competitive Intelligence

Very commercially interesting.

Example:

```text
Company A
    ↓

    ↓

    ↓
Pricing change
    ↓
New partnership
    ↓
Hiring
    ↓
Expansion
    ↓
Market reaction
```

TEXIA can ask:

> What changed in Company A's strategic position?

Investment research workflows already use document intelligence to track management commentary, competitive positioning, risk-factor evolution, capital allocation and guidance versus actual results. ([Orbit Fin Docs][1])

**Verdict: Excellent second-phase laboratory and potentially an important commercial market.**

# 5. Supply Chain Intelligence

This is particularly interesting because it introduces **relationships and networks**.

Example:

```text
Supplier A
     ↓
Component B
     ↓
Factory C
     ↓
Port D
     ↓
Customer E
```

Then:

```text
Supplier problem
       ↓
Production delay
       ↓
Shipping delay
       ↓
Customer shortage
```

Now TEXIA has to understand **propagation**.

This is a major step beyond simply understanding documents.

Supply-network intelligence products already focus on supplier behavior, logistics problems, regulatory changes, political risk and relationship mapping. ([Deloitte][4])

**Verdict: Excellent once we have established the basic engine.**

# 6. Financial / Investment Intelligence

This is probably one of the **most commercially attractive**, but I wouldn't make it our first test.

Why?

Because it introduces additional problems:

```text
Documents
+
Market data
+
Numerical data
+
Forecasts
+
Prices
+
Time series
+
Accounting definitions
+
Market reactions
```

We could very quickly move from:

> "What changed?"

into:

> "What will the price do?"

That's a much harder problem.

Investment workflows already combine document analysis, earnings, guidance, competitive intelligence, supply-chain relationships, portfolio monitoring and event-based analysis. ([Orbit Fin Docs][1])

**Verdict: Very valuable, but don't let finance distort the universal TEXIA architecture too early.**

This is an excellent test of:

```text
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Hypothesis
 ↓
Counter-evidence
 ↓
Consensus
 ↓
Change in understanding
```

For example:

> Has the scientific understanding of technology X changed over the last five years?

We could track:

```text
Paper A
 ↓
Hypothesis

Paper B
 ↓
Supporting evidence

Paper C
 ↓
Contradiction

Paper D
 ↓
New experiment

Current state
 ↓
Unresolved
```

This would strongly test TEXIA's **epistemic architecture**.

**Verdict: Excellent for testing knowledge evolution, but less useful for testing rapid change.**

# 8. Security / Risk Intelligence

Strong application because security events are naturally:

```text
Event
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Timeline
 ↓
Affected entities
 ↓
Impact
 ↓
Response
```

But this category is broad.

Cybersecurity, physical security, corporate risk and fraud each have different specialized requirements.

For example, cyber investigations already emphasize evidence-backed timelines and preserving what each timestamp actually represents rather than inventing a clean narrative. ([Threat Intelligence Lab][5])

**Verdict: Strong, but specialize later.**

# 9. Infrastructure / Physical World

Not:

**Verdict: Great later test, especially if we combine documents with physical/geospatial data.**

If I rank them by **how many TEXIA primitives they force us to use**, I get:

But there's another dimension we should consider.

## We need two different rankings

### Ranking A — Best laboratory

**OSINT → Geopolitics → Regulation**

### Ranking B — Potential commercial opportunity

Potentially:

**Corporate → Financial → Regulatory → Supply Chain → Risk**

Those are **not necessarily the same thing**.

And that's important.

We shouldn't choose our first experiment based on what might make money.

We should first prove:

> **Does TEXIA actually work?**

Then:

> **Where does it create unusually high value?**

Then:

> **Who will pay for that value?**

# My Recommendation for Test 001

I would choose:

## **OSINT / Real-World Investigation**

But **not a generic "OSINT exercise."**

We should find one real-world case where:

```text
          MULTIPLE SOURCES
                 ↓
        ┌────────┼────────┐
        ↓        ↓        ↓
      PEOPLE   EVENTS   ORGANIZATIONS
        │        │        │
        └────────┼────────┘
                 ↓
              CLAIMS
                 ↓
             EVIDENCE
                 ↓
               TIME
                 ↓
             CONFLICTS
                 ↓
            STATE CHANGE
                 ↓
             INFERENCE
                 ↓
           OPEN QUESTIONS
```

We then run **TEXIA V0.1 manually from beginning to end**.

The test should be difficult enough that a normal summary would lose important structure.

And there's a useful precedent for this kind of evaluation: OSINT case labs increasingly structure exercises around real public-source cases, evidence chains, timelines, conflicting information and explicit separation of source, claim, interpretation and missing evidence. ([CSINT Research][6])

### Therefore our sequence should be:

```text
TEXIA V0.1
      ↓
TEST 001 — OSINT / Investigation
      ↓
Run complete manual workflow
      ↓
Measure what worked
      ↓
Identify failures
      ↓
Modify TEXIA
      ↓
TEST 002 — different domain
      ↓
Compare
```

**And only after several real-world applications should we decide which TEXIA application has the strongest product opportunity.**

That keeps us faithful to the rule we established earlier:

> **Don't build the software until we've proven the instrument manually.**
